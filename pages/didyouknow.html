Ghana's Road to Independence

1957 Year of independence
1st In sub-Saharan Africa
6 March Independence Day
Chapter 1

The Gold Coast

Before 1957, Ghana was a British colony called the Gold Coast, named after the gold that Europeans came to trade for. Over time, many people grew tired of colonial rule and began to demand a say in how their own country was run.

Chapter 2

The Call for Change

In 1947, a group of leaders formed the United Gold Coast Convention (UGCC) to push for self-government. They invited Kwame Nkrumah, who had been studying and working abroad, to return home and join them as general secretary.

In February 1948, ex-servicemen marched in Accra to demand their promised benefits and were met with gunfire. The shock of that day led to riots across the country and made it clear that change could not wait.

Chapter 3

From Prison to Power

Nkrumah left the UGCC and formed his own party, the Convention People's Party (CPP), in 1949 with the slogan "Self-government now". In 1950 he led peaceful strikes and protests, and the colonial government jailed him.

In the 1951 election, the CPP won big while he was still in prison. He was released to lead the government, and in 1952 he became Prime Minister of the Gold Coast.

On the night of 5 to 6 March 1957, a new nation was born.

Chapter 4

Midnight, 6 March 1957

Crowds gathered at the Old Polo Grounds in Accra as the clock moved past midnight. Nkrumah declared the Gold Coast free, and the country took the name Ghana, after an ancient West African empire.

The new flag carried red, gold and green with a black star in the middle, a symbol of African freedom. Ghana's success inspired many other African countries to seek their own independence.

Chapter 5

The Big Six

The six leaders arrested after the 1948 riots became known as the Big Six: Kwame Nkrumah, J. B. Danquah, Edward Akufo-Addo, Ebenezer Ako-Adjei, William Ofori Atta and Emmanuel Obetsebi-Lamptey.
